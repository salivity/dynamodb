DynamoDB Provisioned vs On-Demand Capacity Mode

Choosing between Amazon DynamoDB's provisioned and on-demand capacity modes centers on a fundamental trade-off: cost efficiency versus operational simplicity and elasticity. On-demand mode eliminates capacity planning by automatically handling unpredictable traffic spikes at a higher price per request, while provisioned mode provides significantly lower costs for predictable workloads at the risk of throttling and resource management overhead.

The Primary Trade-Off: Cost vs. Elasticity

The central decision rests on how well you can predict your application's traffic patterns and how much you are willing to pay for automatic management:

  • On-Demand Mode prioritizes elasticity and ease of use. You pay strictly for the read and write operations your application performs. There is no capacity forecasting, and DynamoDB instantly accommodates sudden bursts of traffic up to double the previous peak. The trade-off is financial: unit-for-unit, on-demand requests cost roughly five to seven times more than the baseline cost of provisioned units.
  • Provisioned Mode prioritizes cost efficiency and predictability. You configure specific Read Capacity Units (RCUs) and Write Capacity Units (WCUs). When utilization is high and steady, this mode is drastically cheaper, especially when combined with DynamoDB Reserved Capacity. The trade-off is operational complexity and throttling risk: if traffic surges faster than DynamoDB auto-scaling can adjust, surplus requests are throttled and fail.

Understanding On-Demand Mode

On-demand mode functions on a pay-per-request model. It requires no baseline threshold settings.

  • Instant Scaling: It handles sudden, sharp traffic spikes without throttling, provided the surge does not exceed double the previously achieved peak within a short window.
  • Zero Idle Cost: If an application receives zero requests, read and write charges drop to zero (storage charges still apply).
  • Ideal Scenarios: New applications with unknown adoption patterns, highly variable workloads, batch jobs that run intermittently, or serverless architectures with spiky traffic.

Understanding Provisioned Mode

Provisioned mode requires specifying throughput thresholds, often managed via Application Auto Scaling to adjust capacity up or down based on target utilization metrics.

  • Cost Control: Unit costs are much lower. For consistent, 24/7 high-volume applications, provisioned mode yields massive financial savings compared to on-demand.
  • Auto-Scaling Lag: Auto-scaling is reactive rather than instantaneous. It typically takes several minutes to detect a sustained increase and provision additional capacity, which can result in ProvisionedThroughputExceededException errors during sudden traffic spikes.
  • Ideal Scenarios: Applications with well-understood, stable traffic patterns, workloads with gradual growth, or systems operating at a high, steady baseline where cost reduction is a primary operational objective.

Key Decision Factors

To choose the right mode, evaluate your workload against three main criteria:

  1. Traffic Predictability: If traffic is smooth, steady, or ramps up gradually, provisioned capacity is usually the superior choice. If traffic is spiky, bursty, or unpredictable, on-demand protects against throttling.
  2. Average Utilization: If your provisioned capacity operates at less than 15–20% average utilization because you must over-provision to handle peaks, on-demand mode may actually be cheaper despite the higher unit cost.
  3. Management Overhead: Teams wanting zero operational burden regarding capacity management benefit from on-demand mode, trading a higher AWS bill for reduced engineering effort.
