DynamoDB Partition Re-balancing After Bulk Deletion

Amazon DynamoDB manages storage re-balancing after bulk item deletions through an automated, internal lifecycle involving storage engine compaction, partition consolidation, and capacity reallocation. When large volumes of data are deleted—either through manual deletion operations or the Time to Live (TTL) feature—DynamoDB does not instantly shrink allocated physical boundaries. Instead, it reclaims space asynchronously at the storage tier and automatically merges underutilized partitions in the background to optimize routing, capacity, and resource density.

Asynchronous Storage Reclamation and Compaction

At the individual partition level, data is stored across a three-node replication group using SSD-backed B-trees. When items are removed in bulk via DeleteItem or BatchWriteItem, DynamoDB initially marks these records for deletion or modifies the B-tree leaf nodes directly.

The physical disk space is not instantly released back to the underlying operating system. Instead, DynamoDB runs asynchronous background compaction and garbage collection processes. These tasks de-fragment the B-tree nodes, prune deleted data pages, and reclaim unused storage blocks, making the space available for future writes within that partition.

Partition Consolidation and Merging

DynamoDB partitions automatically split when a partition exceeds 10 GB of storage or exceeds its throughput limits (1,000 WCUs or 3,000 RCUs). Historically, partitions could only split and never merged back together.

In modern DynamoDB architecture, AWS employs an automated partition-merging mechanism. When bulk deletions significantly lower the total storage volume or when read/write traffic remains consistently low, the internal storage orchestrator detects adjacent, underutilized partitions. DynamoDB then transparently merges these smaller partitions back into a single partition. This consolidation process:

  • Reclaims excess compute and metadata overhead across the storage fleet.
  • Reduces partition fragmentation.
  • Eliminates the risk of throughput dilution across numerous near-empty partitions.

Impact on Throughput Allocation

Every DynamoDB partition holds a fractional slice of the table's total provisioned or allocated burst throughput. When a table contains numerous empty partitions due to bulk deletion, write and read capacity could theoretically become fragmented across partitions that store little to no data.

DynamoDB's global request routers and partition merging resolve this issue. As underutilized partitions are consolidated, the provisioned throughput (or on-demand capacity allocation) is aggregated back into fewer, denser partitions. This ensures that incoming queries and writes have access to maximum throughput thresholds per partition without causing artificial throttling.

Bulk Manual Deletions vs. Time to Live (TTL)

The mechanism used to perform bulk deletions alters how the internal workload is balanced:

  • Manual Bulk Deletions: Issuing bulk delete requests consumes provisioned Write Capacity Units (WCUs) or on-demand write units. This generates immediate internal replication traffic across the Paxos quorum, updating partition indexes immediately, followed by standard background compaction.
  • Time to Live (TTL): DynamoDB handles TTL expirations as an internal background task. Expired items are identified and cleared without consuming provisioned throughput. Background sweeps mark the items for removal, and the storage engine removes them during subsequent compaction passes, avoiding contention with user-driven read and write workloads.
