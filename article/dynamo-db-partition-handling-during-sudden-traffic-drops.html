DynamoDB Partition Handling During Sudden Traffic Drops

This article examines how Amazon DynamoDB manages partition assignments, provisioned throughput allocation, and partition lifecycles when an application experiences a sudden drop in traffic under provisioned throughput mode. It covers the mechanics of throughput distribution across partitions, how burst capacity behaves during idle periods, the operational realities of partition merging, and the potential throttling risks associated with scaling down capacity across a high partition count.

Throughput Division Across Partitions

DynamoDB distributes provisioned Read Capacity Units (RCUs) and Write Capacity Units (WCUs) equally across all physical partitions supporting a table. Partitions are created automatically as a table grows beyond 10 GB in size, exceeds 1,000 WCUs, or exceeds 3,000 RCUs.

When traffic suddenly drops, the physical partitions do not immediately disappear. Instead, the allocated capacity per partition remains unchanged unless the provisioned capacity settings are modified—either manually or through DynamoDB Auto Scaling. If your table has 10 partitions and 5,000 RCUs, each partition retains an allocation of 500 RCUs, regardless of whether incoming request rates fall to zero.

Idle Capacity and the Burst Pool

During a sudden traffic decline, DynamoDB partitions become largely idle. When a partition does not consume its allocated provisioned throughput, DynamoDB banks unused capacity into a burst pool.

Each partition can accumulate up to 300 seconds (5 minutes) of unused read and write capacity. If sudden traffic drops are followed by intermittent, spiky requests, this accumulated burst capacity allows individual partitions to momentarily exceed their allocated provisioned limit without throttling. However, burst capacity is an ephemeral buffer managed in the background; it should not be relied upon as a permanent scaling mechanism.

The Downscaling Effect and Throughput Dilution

If DynamoDB Auto Scaling is enabled, a prolonged drop in traffic will trigger a scale-down event to reduce costs. Auto Scaling reduces the table’s total provisioned RCUs and WCUs based on your target utilization metrics.

Because provisioned throughput is divided evenly across all existing partitions, scaling down the total throughput decreases the capacity slice assigned to each individual partition. For example, if a table that scaled out to 20 partitions drops from 10,000 WCUs to 200 WCUs, each partition's allocated throughput falls from 500 WCUs to just 10 WCUs.

If traffic unexpectedly rebounds and targets a single partition (a hot partition) before the table can scale back up, that partition can quickly become throttled because its dedicated capacity threshold is now significantly lower.

Partition Merging Behavior

Historically, DynamoDB partitions only split and never merged; once a table created new partitions, that partition count remained permanent.

DynamoDB now includes automated partition merging capabilities in the background. When table size or provisioned throughput decreases significantly and remains low, DynamoDB gradually consolidates adjacent, underutilized physical partitions.

This process is not instantaneous. In the event of a sudden traffic drop, partition merging will not execute immediately to match the instant change in workload. Merging is an asynchronous background maintenance task designed for long-term topology optimization. In the immediate aftermath of a sudden traffic cliff, the table retains its existing partition count, and partition assignment for existing data remains intact.

Global Secondary Indexes (GSIs)

The same partition mechanics apply to Global Secondary Indexes (GSIs). If write traffic to the base table suddenly drops, write consumption on the GSIs will drop proportionally. However, if a GSI was heavily partitioned during peak load and its capacity is scaled down post-drop, the reduced throughput is equally diluted across all GSI partitions. Unbalanced access patterns on the GSI following a sudden drop can result in index throttling, which in turn throttles writes to the primary table.
