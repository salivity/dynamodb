DynamoDB Partition Scaling Under Sustained Traffic

Amazon DynamoDB automatically accommodates sustained increases in application traffic by dynamically creating and redistributing database partitions to maintain low-latency performance. When read or write demands consistently surpass the limits of existing partitions, DynamoDB splits those partitions, allocates throughput, and leverages internal mechanisms like adaptive capacity to balance workloads. Understanding how this automatic scaling behaves over time is essential for designing resilient schemas and preventing performance bottlenecks.

Fundamentals of DynamoDB Partitions

DynamoDB stores table data across physical storage units known as partitions, which are managed automatically by AWS. A single partition has fixed upper thresholds:

  • A maximum storage capacity of 10 GB.
  • A throughput limit of 1,000 Write Capacity Units (WCU).
  • A throughput limit of 3,000 Read Capacity Units (RCU).

When a table is created, DynamoDB allocates an initial number of partitions based on the requested provisioned throughput or the initial data size.

How DynamoDB Detects and Handles Sustained Increases

When traffic increases gradually or remains high over an extended period, DynamoDB reacts through a series of automated scaling steps:

1. Adaptive Capacity Intervention

DynamoDB uses adaptive capacity to address short-term spikes and uneven traffic patterns. If an individual partition experiences disproportionate traffic (a "hot" partition), adaptive capacity instantly draws unused throughput from quieter partitions to satisfy the demand without throttling, provided the total table throughput is not exceeded.

2. Partition Splitting

If the sustained demand on a single partition approaches or exceeds physical hardware limits (1,000 WCU or 3,000 RCU) over time, or if table-wide provisioned or on-demand throughput increases beyond the sum of existing partition maximums, DynamoDB triggers a partition split.

During a split:

  • The original partition's key space is divided into two halves.
  • The data belonging to each half is migrated to two distinct, newly allocated partitions.
  • The throughput previously assigned to the original partition is now split evenly between the two child partitions.
  • The operation occurs asynchronously in the background with zero downtime and no impact on availability.

3. Scaling in On-Demand vs. Provisioned Modes

  • On-Demand Mode: DynamoDB tracks previous traffic peaks. If sustained traffic doubles the previous peak, the system immediately scales by splitting partitions to establish a new performance baseline. It accommodates up to double the previous peak instantly, while splitting partitions behind the scenes to prepare for higher sustained volume.
  • Provisioned Capacity Mode: Increasing the table’s provisioned RCU or WCU settings triggers partition provisioning if the total required capacity exceeds the aggregate capacity of the existing partitions.

Long-Term Behavior of Partition Scaling

As sustained traffic increases over weeks or months, the table’s partition count scales upward. Key characteristics of long-term partition behavior include:

  • Partitions Do Not Automatically Merge Down: Historically, as a table scales up its partition count to satisfy peak sustained workloads, those partitions remain in place even if traffic later declines. The table maintains the higher partition count to ensure it can handle return traffic without re-triggering partition splits.
  • Uniform Key Distribution Becomes Critical: Because overall provisioned throughput is distributed across all partitions, having a large number of partitions means each individual partition receives a smaller slice of base throughput. Although adaptive capacity helps mitigate this, high-cardinality partition keys remain critical to distribute traffic evenly across the expanded partition fleet.
  • Global Secondary Indexes (GSIs): GSIs scale their partitions independently of the base table. Sustained traffic on queries targeting a GSI will cause the index's partitions to split independently to match read and write demands.
